01 · About

Who I am and how I built these skills

I am a business student working toward a career in enterprise and product management. Across this module I worked in a team of four to take a new venture from a rough idea to a panel pitch. That process gave me a structured way to build and show real employability skills, rather than list them on a CV.

This page sets out the skills I chose to develop, the activities I used to build them, and the evidence I collected for each one. Every skill links to a learning outcome for the module (LO4): demonstrate attainment of employability skills in developing and presenting a new venture.

I treated the whole project as a portfolio. I kept a weekly log, saved drafts and feedback, and photographed or exported each artefact as I produced it. The result is the evidence shown further down.

How to read this portfolio

  1. Identify — I picked eight skills that a new venture needs.
  2. Engage — I joined every module activity to build them.
  3. Collect — I gathered posters, models, charts and photos.
  4. Present — I uploaded that evidence to this webpage.
02 · Context

The new venture: EcoBite

EcoBite sells low-cost meal boxes made from surplus food that campus caterers and local shops would otherwise throw away. Students get a £3.50 fresh meal; suppliers cut waste and recover value.

The venture is the spine of this portfolio. Each employability skill below was developed by doing a real task for EcoBite — researching the market, modelling the money, building a prototype, and pitching to a panel. The venture gave the skills a purpose and gave me evidence worth showing.

04 · Evidence

Evidence for each skill

Each block shows an artefact I produced, what I did, and what I learned.

Research & analysis

Market research report

I surveyed 142 students, ran three competitor price audits, and built four customer personas. The data showed a clear gap and a price point students would pay.

What I learned: evidence beats opinion. Decisions held up in the pitch because they were backed by numbers.

Communication & presenting

Panel pitch

I delivered an 8-minute pitch to a judging panel, then handled unscripted questions. I rehearsed four times and cut my slides from 18 to 12 for clarity.

What I learned: less content, said well, lands harder than more content read out.

Teamwork

Team workshops

Our team of four agreed roles early and made decisions by consensus, with a vote when we were stuck. We ran structured peer feedback after each milestone.

What I learned: clear roles stop duplication and make disagreement productive.

Leadership & initiative

Leading the project rhythm

I chaired weekly stand-ups, set agendas, and volunteered to lead the live pitch. When scope slipped, I called a vote to re-focus on the core idea.

What I learned: leadership here meant keeping momentum, not controlling people.

Creativity & innovation

Packaging prototype

I built a low-fidelity meal-box prototype and tested it with 12 users. Their feedback changed the label and the portion size before the pitch.

What I learned: a rough prototype gets better feedback than a perfect description.

Commercial awareness

Business Model Canvas

I mapped partners, revenue and cost structure on a canvas and iterated it twice. It exposed a weak point in supply reliability that we then designed around.

What I learned: a model on one page reveals gaps a written plan hides.

Numeracy & finance

Financial model

I built a six-month forecast in Excel with formulas and a break-even line. It set our funding ask and showed when the venture turns a gross profit.

What I learned: I can now defend numbers, not just produce them.

Planning & time management

Project plan

I ran the project on a 10-week Gantt chart and a Trello board. Tasks had owners and deadlines, which kept the team on track to the pitch date.

What I learned: planning backward from the deadline removed the last-week panic.

06 · Reflection

Reflection and action plan

What went well

I grew most in communication and planning. Rehearsing the pitch out loud, and owning the financial model, moved me from nervous to confident. Keeping a weekly log meant I could see progress instead of guessing at it.

What I would change

I left prototype testing late, so some feedback arrived after decisions were fixed. Next time I will test earlier and leave room to act on what I find.

Next steps

  • Join a student enterprise society to keep pitching.
  • Take a short finance-modelling course to reach level 5.
  • Seek a placement where I present to real clients.
  • Keep this portfolio updated as a live CV.
